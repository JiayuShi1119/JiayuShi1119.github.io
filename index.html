About Me

Where electronics meet the physical world

How a curiosity about moving things turned into a career in circuits.

My path into Electrical Engineering began with a simple question: how can materials and electronics work together to make things move? I started in Materials Science and Engineering at the Sichuan University–Pittsburgh Institute (SCUPI), then switched to Electrical and Computer Engineering and earned a 3.82/4.0 GPA. Because of that start, I see a circuit as the nervous system that brings a machine, a sensor, or a smart material to life.

I like solving problems from the ground up. I have programmed microcontrollers at the lowest level, designed multi-layer circuit boards, and rebuilt a 3D printer so it could print experimental materials. During a summer internship at Shenzhen Bestsoon Electronic Technology, I learned how chip-assembly machines are run and maintained on a real factory floor. Since transferring to Georgia Tech in Fall 2026, I have been designing circuit boards for the RoboJackets robot soccer team.

Good engineering also depends on people. As President of the SCUPI Student Council, I led 55 members, managed a $12,000 annual budget, and turned 313 pieces of student feedback into real campus changes. Outside the lab, I play guitar and ride long gravel routes around Atlanta.

★ Discovery Project Feature

A Custom 3D Printer for Light-Powered Jumping Soft Robots

Role Project Leader Team 5 students from 4 majors Advisor Prof. Wenwen Xu Timeline Mar 2025 – Present
In short

No 3D printer on the market could print the soft robots we had in mind, so my team built one. The result: tiny printed parts that jump up to 10 times their own length when UV light hits them.

10×Jump height vs. body length
820×Push compared with its own weight
$240Total printer hardware cost

01The Problem

Imagine a robot smaller than a coin that jumps when you shine a light on it, with no wires and no batteries. Robots like this could one day work in very tight spaces, even inside the human body. Materials that bend under light already exist, but they usually move slowly, push weakly, and can only be made as thin, flat films.

To build a robot that truly jumps, we needed a single 3D-printed part that combines a stiff base, a flexible hinge, a light-sensitive layer, and a spring-like latch that stores energy and releases it in a split second. No printer we could buy could make that part. It had to print liquid inks that dry quickly, and it had to blend two materials smoothly so the stiff and soft sections would not peel apart.

02The Process

I led a five-person team from electrical, materials, mechanical, and industrial engineering. We split the work into four connected pieces: the material, the machine, the software, and the final structure.

  1. Building the printer. Starting from an open-source frame, I built a printer with two syringe nozzles for about $240 in parts (Figs. 4–5). I added a heated aluminum bed with a temperature sensor so the inks dry evenly, built a leveling platform that keeps the nozzle height steady to within 0.0008 in (0.02 mm), about a quarter of the width of a human hair, and moved the heavy electronics lower so the frame stopped wobbling.
  2. Writing the software. Normal 3D-printing software can only control one material at a time. We wrote our own program that takes a regular print file and splits every movement between the two syringes, so the mix can shift gradually from one material to the other. Its desktop app (Fig. 3) previews the blend, corrects for the small delay between the two syringes, and sends the job straight to the printer.
  3. Tuning the ink. With my materials teammates, I helped mix light-sensitive inks, and we tested many print speeds and temperatures until the crystals in the ink grew neatly lined up with the printing direction (Fig. 7).

03The Outcome

The finished printer lines up its two nozzles to within 0.004 in (0.1 mm) and can print smooth blends between materials in a single job (Fig. 6). The small actuators we printed jump again and again under UV light (Fig. 2): up to 10 times their own body length, pushing with a force about 820 times their own weight.

Beyond the numbers, I learned how to lead teammates from different majors toward one goal, and how electrical choices, such as where a sensor goes, which motor driver to use, and how power is laid out, directly limit what a materials scientist can achieve. The team is now preparing a software copyright, a patent application for the printer, and a journal paper.

Technical details for engineers
  • Controller: BigTreeTech Octopus Pro with TMC2209 stepper drivers; hollow aluminum bed with a heater and a platinum RTD sensor.
  • Slicer: rewrites single-nozzle G-code for two synchronized extruders, splitting each move as E = E1 + E2; four modes (basic, path optimization, zipper gradient, formula gradient); lag-volume calibration; serial-port printing; compatible with Cura and Pronterface.
  • Inks: azobenzene (DR13) chromophores, PVP, and fumed silica; meniscus-guided crystallization in binary solvents such as 8:2 DMF:ethanol at 194–212 °F (90–100 °C); feed rates F2.2–F5.0.
  • Actuator: latch-mediated spring actuation (snap-through instability); work density about 40,000 J/m³.

Project

Multi-Sensor Circuit Board for Soccer Robots

Team RoboJackets RoboCup (Electrical) Role Hardware Designer Timeline Fall 2026 – Present
In short

I designed a small circuit board that gives our soccer robots six kinds of sensing and can be chained to other boards with a single cable. The layout is now essentially finished: all 106 connections are routed.

01The Problem

Robot soccer players need to know how they are moving, which way they face, and what is around them, all without a tangle of wires. The team needed a compact, reliable sensor board that could link to other boards in a chain and safely take power from either a USB cable or the board before it.

02The Process

I designed the board in KiCad around a small RP2040 microcontroller and connected six parts: a motion sensor, a temperature sensor, a magnetic sensor, a switch reader, a color sensor, and a driver for eight RGB lights. A small power-selection circuit picks USB or chain power without letting the two fight each other. I used four copper layers, with the two inner layers for power and ground, to keep signals clean on a board about the size of a credit card (3.15 × 1.97 in).

03The Outcome

The layout is essentially finished (Figs. 8–9). All 106 nets are routed with none left unconnected, using about 800 track segments and 269 vias to move signals between the four layers. Next, I will do a final design review, send the board out for manufacturing, then write the startup code and test each sensor before the competition season.

Technical details for engineers
  • RP2040 with external QSPI flash; SPI in/out for daisy-chaining, with a growing data packet passed to the next board.
  • I²C devices: ICM-42605 IMU, LM75 temperature sensor, TMAG5273 Hall-effect sensor, TCA9534 GPIO expander with DIP switch, VEML3328 color sensor, IS31FL3254 LED driver.
  • Power: LM66100 ideal-diode OR-ing between USB and chain 5 V, followed by an AP2112K 3.3 V LDO.
  • 4-layer stack-up (F.Cu / In1.Cu / In2.Cu / B.Cu) with internal 3.3 V and GND planes; tight QFN fan-out; 80 × 50 mm outline.
  • Board statistics: 424 pads, 106 nets, 805 track segments, 269 vias, 0 unrouted.

Project

Programming a Microcontroller from the Ground Up

Role Project Leader Advisor Prof. Fashu Xu Timeline Mar – Jul 2026
In short

Instead of using ready-made code libraries, we programmed an STM32 chip directly, so we understood and controlled every step it took.

01The Problem

Ready-made code libraries make microcontrollers easier to program, but they hide what the chip is really doing and can add small delays. In machines that must react on time, those hidden details matter. Our goal was to control the chip directly, one setting at a time.

02The Process

I led the code design, writing in C and assembly language to set up the chip’s clock, its input and output pins, and its interrupts, the signals that let the chip react instantly to events. On top of that foundation we built a keypad reader, a stepper-motor controller, and a sensor connection, and we checked the timing of every signal with an oscilloscope.

03The Outcome

The finished system responded quickly and reliably to every input. The project gave me the low-level confidence I now use when bringing a new circuit board to life for the first time.

Technical details for engineers
  • STM32L476 (ARM Cortex-M4) programmed in Keil µVision 5 with mixed C and ARM assembly, no HAL.
  • Register-level clock-tree, GPIO, and NVIC/EXTI interrupt configuration.
  • Matrix keypad scanning, stepper-motor phase sequencing, and an I²C sensor interface, verified with a digital storage oscilloscope.

Project

Hand-Wired Digital Logic Circuits

Course Digital Circuits & Systems Lab School SCUPI
In short

I turned logic designs on paper into working circuits on a breadboard, and built a step-by-step debugging habit I still use on every board.

01The Problem

A logic circuit that works on paper often fails on the bench because of a loose wire, an unconnected input, or a timing issue. The lab challenge was to turn truth tables and state diagrams into hardware that actually works.

02The Process

I simplified each design on paper, mapped it onto standard logic chips, and wired it on a breadboard, using switches and push buttons as inputs and LEDs as outputs. I labeled key points in the circuit, tested one block at a time, and tracked down faults with a logic probe and a multimeter.

03The Outcome

Every circuit matched its truth table and state sequence. More importantly, I built a debugging habit, isolate, measure, then fix, that I now use on every board I build.

Technical details for engineers
  • Boolean algebra and Karnaugh-map minimization; combinational and sequential (state-machine) designs.
  • Discrete 74-series logic ICs, DIP-switch and push-button inputs, LED outputs; fault tracing with a logic probe and multimeter.

Guitar & Lutherie Acoustics

I am a fingerstyle and acoustic guitarist and have performed live at the Xiancao Music Festival. What fascinates me most is the physics inside the instrument: how the type of wood and the pattern of braces under the top shape the way a guitar resonates and sounds.

To me, a guitar top is a beautifully tuned mechanical filter—the same ideas I study in signals and systems, carved in wood.

Endurance Road & Gravel Cycling

I founded and captained a 14-member collegiate cycling team, organized the Longquan Mountain Challenge, and completed a 310-mile (500 km) endurance tour around Hainan Island. Today I explore Atlanta’s road and gravel routes on a 700c × 40c gravel bike.

Long rides teach pacing, preparation, and calm problem solving when something breaks far from home.

Travel & Exploration

Travel is how I recharge. I have hiked the river valleys and canola fields of Qinghai’s Hainan Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, fed seagulls on Dianchi Lake in Kunming, kayaked the lake below Mount Wawu in Sichuan, and watched the sun set over the sea on Pingtan Island in Fujian.
